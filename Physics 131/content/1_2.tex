
\subsection{The Electric Field}
	\subsubsection{Definition}
		The fundamental problem that electrodynamic hopes to solve is this:
		We have some collection of electric charges $q_1,q_2,q_3,\ldots$, called
		\textit{source charges}; What force do they exert on another charge $q_0$, called
		\textit{text charge}? The positions of the source charges are given (all as functions of time)
		and the trajectory of the test particle is \textit{to be calculated}. In general,
		both the source charges and test charge are in motion.

		The solution to this problem is facilitated by the \textbf{principle of superposition},
		which states that the interaction between any two charges is completely unnafected by
		the presence of others. That means that to determine the force on $q_0$, we must first 
		determine the force $\vb{F}_1$ due to $q_1$ alone, then $q_2$, and so on. Finally, we take 
		the vector sum of these individual forces.
		\[
			\vb{F} = \sum_i \vb{F}_i = \vb{F}_1 + \vb{F}_2 + \vb{F}_3 + \cdots
		\]
		Thus, if we can find the force on $q_0$ due to a \textit{single} source charge $q$, we 
		are, in principle, done, and the rest is just repitition.

		A priori, this seems easy. However, upon further study, we find that the force on $q_0$
		not only depends on the separation distance $r$ between the charges, but also depends on
		both their velocities and on the acceleration of $q$. Moreover, it is not those states 
		at this instant that matters, but the state that $q$ had in an earlier time, since information
		travels at a finite speed $c$, and we can only grasp what happened after the information reached 
		us.

		Therefore, in spite of the fact that the basic question (``What is the force on
		$q_0$ due to $q$?'') is easy to state, it does not pay to confront it head on; rather, we
		shall go at it by stages. In the meantime, the theory we develop will allow for the
		solution of more subtle electromagnetic problems that do not present themselves
		in quite this simple format. To begin with, we shall consider the special case
		of \textbf{electrostatics} in which all the \textit{source charges are stationary} (though the test
		charge may be moving).
	\subsubsection{Coulomb's Law}
		The force on a test charge $q_0$ due to a single point charge $q$, that is at \textit{rest} a 
		distance $r$ away, is given (based on experiments) by \textbf{Coulomb's Law},
		a fundamental principle first established by Charles-Augustin de Coulomb in 1785 using a torsion balance experiment.
		
		It is expressed as the following:
		\begin{definition}{Coulomb's Law}
			\begin{equation}
				\vb{F} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q_0 q}{r^2}\vu{r} 
			\end{equation}
		\end{definition}
		The constant $\varepsilon_0$ is called the \textbf{permitivity of free space}. In
		SI units, it is defined as 
		\begin{definition}{Permitivity of free space}
		    \begin{equation}
				\varepsilon_0 = \SI{8.85e-12}{C^2/Nm^2}
		    \end{equation}
		\end{definition}
		In other words, then, the force is proportional to the product of the charges,
		and inversely proportional to the square of their distances\footnote{Newton much?}.
		The vector $\vb{r}$ is the separation vector from $\vb{x}$, the location of $q$, and to $\vb{x}_0$,
		the location of the $q_0$ 
		\[
			\vb{r} = \vb{x} - \vb{x}_0
		\]
		and $r$ is the magnitude of this separation vector, $r=\norm{\vb{r}}$. The 
		force points along the line from $q$ to $q_0$; it is repulsive if $q_0$ and 
		$q$ are the same sign, and attractive if their signs are the opposite.
		
		Coulomb’s law and the principle of superposition constitute the physical input
		for electrostatics—the rest, except for some special properties of matter, is 
		mathematical elaboration of these fundamental rules.

	\subsubsection{The Electric Field}
		If we have \textit{several} point charges $q_1,q_2,q_3,\ldots,q_n$, at distances
		$r_1,r_2,r_3,\ldots,r_n$ from $q_0$, then the total force on $q_0$ is trivially
		\begin{align*}
			\vb{F} &= \vb{F}_1 + \vb{F}_2 + \vb{F}_3 + \cdots \\
				   &= \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \qty(
						\frac{q_0q_1}{r_1^2}\vu{r}_1 + 
						\frac{q_0q_2}{r_2^2}\vu{r}_2 + 
						\frac{q_0q_3}{r_3^2}\vu{r}_3 + 
					  \cdots )\\
				   &= \frac{q_0}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \qty(
						\frac{q_1}{r_1^2}\vu{r}_1 + 
						\frac{q_2}{r_2^2}\vu{r}_2 + 
						\frac{q_3}{r_3^2}\vu{r}_3 + 
					  \cdots )\\
		\end{align*}
		or
		\begin{equation}
		    \vb{F} = q_0 \vb{E}
		\end{equation}
		where
		\begin{definition}{Discrete definition of the Electric field}
			\begin{equation} \label{eq: discrete E}
				\vb{E}(\vb{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{q_i}{r_i^2}\vu{r}_i  
			\end{equation}
		\end{definition}
		$\vb{E}$ is called the \textbf{electric field} of the source charges. Notice that it is 
		a function of position $(\vb{r})$ because the separation vectors $\vb{r}$ depend on the location of 
		the field point $P$. But it makes no reference to the test charge $q$. The electric field is a vector
		quantity which varies from point to point and is determined by the configuration of 
		source charges. Physically ,$\vb{E}(\vb{r})$ is the force per unit charge that would
		be exerted on a text charge if you were to place one on $P$.

		The principle of superposition also means that the electric field produced by a
		collection of charges can be determined independently of any other charges that
		may subsequently be introduced into the system. Each source charge contributes its 
		own electric field at the point $P$, and the total field is simply the vector sum
		of these individual contributions. This is particularly useful because the field 
		can be calculated once for a given configuration of source charges and then used
		to determine the force on any test charge placed within that field.
		\newpage
	\subsubsection{Continuus Charge Distribution}
		Our definition of the electric field (\ref{eq: discrete E}) assumes that the source
		of the fields is a collection of discrete point charges $q_i$ (Fig \ref{subfig: efield ptcharge}). If instead, the charge
		is distributed continuously over some region, the sum thus becomes an integral
		\begin{equation}
			\vb{E}(\vb{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int \frac{1}{r^2}\vu{r}\dd{q} 
		\end{equation}
		There are multiple ways where in charges can be distributed over a region.
		In essence, for every dimension (0,1,2,3), there is a corresponding region 
		where charges can be distributed, and where charges can be effectively calculated.
		\begin{figure}[H]
			\centering
			\subfloat[Point charge]{
			\includegraphics[width=1.75in]{images/pointcharge.pdf}
			\label{subfig: efield ptcharge}
			}
			\subfloat[Line charge]{
			\includegraphics[width=1.75in]{images/linecharge2.pdf}
			\label{subfig: efield linecharge}
			}
			\hspace{0.5em}
			\subfloat[Surface charge]{
			\includegraphics[width=1.75in]{images/surfacecharge.pdf}
			\label{subfig: efield surfcharge}
			}
			\hspace{0.5em}
			\subfloat[Volume charge]{
			\includegraphics[width=1.75in]{images/volumecharge.pdf}
			\label{subfig: efield volcharge}
			}
			\caption{Charge distributions}
			\label{fig: charge distributions}
		\end{figure}
		If the charge is spread out along a \textit{line} (Fig \ref{subfig: efield linecharge}), with the charge-per-unit-length $\lambda$,
		then $dq = \lambda \dd{l}$, where $dl$ is a length element along the line. If the charge
		is smeared over a \textit{surface} (Fig \ref{subfig: efield surfcharge}), with charge-per-unit-area $\sigma$, then $dq = \sigma \dd{S}$,
		where $dS$ is an area element on the surface. And if the charge fills a \textit{volume} (Fig \ref{subfig: efield volcharge}),
		with charge-per-unit-volume $\rho$, then $dq = \rho \dd{V}$, where $dV$ is a volume element.
		\[
			dq \to \lambda dl \sim \sigma dS \sim \rho dV
		\]
		Thus, the electric field of a line charge is 
		\begin{equation}
			\vb{E}(\vb{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \int \frac{\lambda(\vb{r})}{r^2} \vu{r} \dd{l} ;
		\end{equation}
		For a surface charge, we have
		\begin{equation}
			\vb{E}(\vb{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \int \frac{\sigma(\vb{r})}{r^2} \vu{r} \dd{S} ;
		\end{equation}
		And for a volume charge
		\begin{definition}{Continuum definition of the Electric Field}
			\begin{equation} \label{eq: cont E}
				\vb{E}(\vb{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \int \frac{\rho(\vb{r})}{r^2} \vu{r} \dd{V} ;
			\end{equation}
		\end{definition}
		Equation \ref{eq: cont E} is often times referred to as ``Coulomb's Law'' because it is such
		a short step from the original, and because a volume charge is in a sense the most general
		and realisitic case. 

		Note carefully the meaning of $\vb{r}$ in these formulae. In general, $\vb{r}$ pertains to the 
		vector from the source charge $q$ to the field point $P$, which means, the meain distance from $\dd{l},\dd{S},\dd{V}$ from
		the point $P$. Although, since these regions are differentials, they reduce to almost pointlike
		at the limits, so the analogy of \textit{distance from the source point to the field point} still stands.

	\subsubsection{Divergence and Curl of Electrostatic Fields}
		We begin with the simplest possible case of electrostatics. A single point
		$q$, situataed at the origin.
		\[
			\vb{E}(\vb{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q}{r^2} \vu{r} 
		\]
		We then have a plot which looks akin to this, the field lines of 
		this point source\footnote{Unfortunately \texttt{matplotlib} does not have a convenient field line functionality so we have to do \texttt{streamplot} for now}
		\begin{figure}[h]
			\centering
			\includegraphics[width=1.75in]{../code/efield.pdf}
			\caption{Electric field lines}
			\label{fig:efieldlines}
		\end{figure}
		These field lines are indicative of the strength of the field. More field lines mean
		a stronger field, denser field lines imply larger field strength, and so on. Such
		diagrams are convinient for representing more complicated fields. Field lines begin
		from positive charges and end on negative charges, and cannot simply terminate mid-field,
		though, they might extend from infinity. Moreover, field lines can never cross,
		% (if they did, the divergence of {$\vb{E}$} would not be zero, and (as we shall soon see) that cannot happen in empty space)
		for at such an intersection, the field would have two different directions at once. 
		With all these in mind, it is easy to sketch out the field of any simple configuration of point charges.
		Begin by drawing the line from the positive, calling it a \textit{source}, and ending it on
		the negative point, the \textit{sink}.

		In this model, the \textit{flux} of $\vb E$ through a surface $S$
		\[
			\Phi_E = \int_S \vb{E}\cdot \dd{\vb S}
		\]
		is a measure of the number of field lines passing through $S$\footnote{\protect{$\dd{\vb S}$} in this case pertains to $\vu{n} \dd{S}$}.
		Though in this notes, we will mainly be showing stream plots, which show the flow of the vector field,
		instead of actual field lines, due to aforementioned limitations. However, in general, the strength
		of the field is proportional to the number of lines through a given sampling area. The dot product $\vb E \cdot \dd{\vb S}$
		picks out the components of $d\vb{S}$ which is along the direction of $\vb{E}$ 
		
		\begin{figure}[H]
		    \centering
		    \subfloat[Opposite charges]{
				\includegraphics[width=1.75in]{images/oppositecharges.pdf}	
		    \label{subfig:opposite charges}
		    }
		    \hspace{0.5em}
		    \subfloat[Equal charges]{
				\includegraphics[width=1.75in]{images/equalcharges.pdf}	
		    \label{subfig:equal charges}
		    }
		    \caption{Charge interactions}
		    \label{fig:charge interactions}
		\end{figure}
			
		

		This suggests that the flux through any closed surface is a measure of the
		total charge inside. For the field lines that originate on a positive charge must
		either pass out through the surface or else terminate on a negative charge inside (Fig \ref{subfig:opposite charges}).
		On the other hand, a charge outside the surface will contribute
		nothing to the total flux, since its field lines pass in one side and out the other (Fig \ref{subfig:equal charges}).
		This is the \textit{essence} of Gauss’s law.

		Quantitatively, this can be expressed as the following:
		Suppose a point charge $q$ at the origin. The flux of $\vb E$ through a spherical surface of radius $r$ is:
		\begin{align}
			\oint \vb{E}\cdot\dd{\vb S}  
			&= \iint \frac{1}{4\pi \varepsilon_0} \pqty{\frac{q}{r^2}\vu{r}}\cdot\pqty{r^2\sin\theta\dd{\theta}\dd{\phi}\vu{r}} \\
			&= \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \cdot 4\pi q \\
			&= \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}q 
		\end{align}
		Notice that the radius of the sphere cancels out, for while the surface area goes up as $r^2$, the field strength
		goes down as $\frac{1}{r^2}$, and as such the product is constant, regardless of the size of the sphere. 
		While we approach this from a spherical picture centered at the origin, it is notable that any closed
		surface, regardless of its shape, would be pierced by the same number of field lines. As such, 
		the flux through any surface is constant, $q/\varepsilon_0$.

		Now, suppose that instead of a single charge at the origin, we have a group
		of charges scattered around. According to the principle of superposition,
		the total field is the sum of all the individual fields.
		\[
			\vb{E} = \sum_i \vb{E}_i
		\]
		Thus, the flux through a surface that encloses them all is 
		\begin{align*}
			\oint \vb{E}\cdot\dd{\vb S} 
			&= \sum_i \pqty{\oint \vb{E}_i \cdot \dd{\vb S}}\\
			&= \sum_i \pqty{\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}q_i}
		\end{align*}
		For any closed surface, then
		\begin{equation}
			\oint\vb{E}\cdot\dd{\vb S} = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}Q_\text{enc} 
		\end{equation}
		where $Q_\text{enc}$ is the total charge enclosed within the surface. This is the 
		quantitative statement of Gauss' law.

		As it stands, Gauss's law is an \textit{integral} equation, but one can easily
		turn it into a \textit{differential} one by applying the divergence theorem
		\begin{equation}
		    \oint_S \vb{E}\cdot \dd{\vb S} = \int_V\pqty{\div\vb{E}}\dd{V}
		\end{equation}
		We rewrite $Q_\text{enc}$ in terms of the charge density $\rho$, we have
		\begin{equation}
			Q_\text{enc} = \int_V \rho \dd{V}
		\end{equation}
		So Gauss's law becomes
		\begin{equation}
			\int_V (\div\vb E)\dd{V} = \int_V \pqty{\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}}\dd{V}
		\end{equation}
		Since this holds for any volume, this implies that the integrands are equal, hence:
		\begin{theorem}{Gauss's Law}
			\begin{equation}
				\div\vb E = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0} 
			\end{equation}
		\end{theorem}
		This is Gauss's law in differential form. The differential version is more compact and tidier,
		but the integral form has the advantage in that it accomodates point, line, and surface charges
		more naturally.

		\subsubsubsection{The Divergence of $\vb{E}$}
			Let us then return and calculate the divergence of $\vb E$ diretly from Eq. \ref{eq: cont E}
			\begin{equation}
				\vb{E}(\vb r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int \frac{\rho(\vb{r})}{r^2}\vu{r}\dd{V}  
			\end{equation}
			Note that $\vb{r} = \vb{x} - \vb{q}$, where $\vb{x}$ is the point being evaluated and $\vb{q}$ is the
			source point. We note the $\vb{x}$ independence in $\vb{r}$. We apply the divergence operator on this field. Thus:
			\begin{equation}
				\div \vb E
				= 
				\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\div\pqty{\frac{\vu{r}}{r^2}}\rho(\vb r)\dd{V} 
			\end{equation}
			Using Eq. \ref{eq: divergence of inverse quadratic}, we know that
			\[
				\div(\frac{\vu{r}}{r^2}) = 4\pi\delta^3(\vb{r})
			\]
			Thus
			\begin{align*}
				\div\vb E 
				&= \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int 4\pi\delta^3(\vb x - \vb q) \rho(\vb q) \dd{V} \\
				&= \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\rho(\vb{x}) 
			\end{align*}
			This is simply Gauss's law in differential form. To recover the integral form, we run the 
			previous steps in reverse, integrating over a volume and applying the divergence theorem
			\begin{equation}
				\int_V \div \vb{E}\dd{V} = \oint \vb{E}\cdot \dd{\vb S} = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0} \int_V \rho \dd{V} = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0} Q_\text{enc}  
			\end{equation}
		\subsubsubsection{The Curl of $\vb E$}
			We calculate the curl of $\vb E$ by studying first the simplest possible configuration: a point 
			charge at the origin. In this case, we have
			\[
				\vb{ E } = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsz} \frac{q}{r^2}\vu{r} 
			\]
			A single glance at Fig. \ref{fig:efieldlines} should tell one that the curl of this field 
			has to be zero. However, we aim to be rigourous, and so, suppose we calculate the 
			line integral of this field from some point $\vb a$ to another point $\vb b$. 
			\[
				\int_{\vb{a}}^{\vb{b}} \vb{E}\cdot\dd{\vb*{\ell}}
			\]
			In spherical coordinates, $\dd{\vb* \ell}=\dd{r}\vu{r}+ r\dd{\theta}\vu*{\theta}+r\sin\theta\dd{\phi}\vu*{\phi}$, therefore
			\[
				\vb{E}\cdot\dd{\vb* \ell} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsz}\frac{q}{r^2}\dd{r} 
			\]
			Therefore:
			\begin{align*}
			    \int_{\vb a}^{\vb b}\vb{E}\cdot\dd{\vb* \ell}
				&= \frac{1}{4\pi\epsz} \int_{\vb a}^{\vb b} \frac{q}{r^2}\dd{r}\\
				&= -\frac{1}{4\pi\epsz}\eval{\frac{q}{r}}_{r_a}^{r_b}\\
				&= \frac{1}{4\pi\epsz}\pqty{
					\frac{q}{r_a}
					-
					\frac{q}{r_b} 
				} 
			\end{align*}
			where $r_a$ is the distance of the origin from $\vb a$ and $r_b$ is
			the distance of the origin from $\vb b$. The integral around a \textit{closed}
			path is evidently zero (because $r_a=r_b$), hence:
			\begin{equation}
				\label{eq:ointEdl=0}	
				\oint \vb{E}\cdot\dd{\vb* \ell} = 0
			\end{equation}
			from this, we apply Stokes's theorem
			\begin{equation}
				\curl\vb{E} = \vb{0}
			\end{equation}
			Proving this for a single point charge at the origin would suffice, but 
			it all seems rather arbitrary. However, these results should hold no matter 
			\textit{where} the charge is located. Moreover, if there are multiple charges,
			the principle of superposition states that the total field is the vector sum 
			of all the individual fields. Thus, we have
			\begin{align*}
			    \curl\vb{E} 
				&=\curl\sum_i \vb{E}_i\\
				&=\curl\pqty{\vb{E}_1 + \vb{E}_2 + \cdots}\\
				&=(\curl \vb{E}_1) + (\curl \vb{E}_2) + \cdots\\
				&=\vb{0} + \vb{0} + \cdots\\
				&=\vb{0}
			\end{align*}
			Hence, we have proven that the curl of an electrostatic field,
			as a consequence of Coulomb's law, is zero everywhere. 
			From this we have the electrostatic condition
			\begin{theorem}{Electrostatic condition}
			   \begin{equation}
				   \curl\vb E = \vb{0}
			   \end{equation} 
			\end{theorem}
			
			
			\begin{remark}
			    We can also prove this by showing that the electric field arises from
				the gradient of a scalar field potential $\vb E = -\grad \Phi$ by the 
				inverse quadratic nature of a point source, and as such is conservative. From
				theorem \ref{thm:irrotational-fields}, it is then trivial to show
				that $\curl\vb E = \vb{0}$.
			\end{remark}

	\subsubsection{Dipoles}
		As another part of what we need to prove in the course notes, we have the following problem.

		\vspace{1em}\hrule\vspace{0.6em}
			Consider a dipole with charge $q$ with separation distance $a$ and dipole moment $p=qa$. Evaluate the electric
			potential and electric field due to this configuration. Then take the point dipole limit $a\to0$ with $p$ finite.
			Find the far-field electric potential and electric field.
		\vspace{1em}\hrule\vspace{1em}

		\paragraph{Solution} Suppose that the dipole is oriented along the $z$-axis with $+q$ at $z=+a/2$ and $-q$ at $z=-a/2$.
		Then:
		\begin{equation}
			\vb{p} = qa\vu{z}
		\end{equation}
		Let this observation point be described by spherical coordinates $(r,\theta,\phi)$, where $\theta$ describes the angle
		measured from the positive dipole axis.

		We show the following
		\begin{enumerate}
			\item \textbf{Potential field}

				  We find the distances of the observation points to the positive and negative charges. 
				  Let $r_+$ be the distance of the observation point from the positive charge, and $r_-$ be
				  its distance from the negative charge. We evaluate from the origin's perspective.
				  From the cosine law, we have
				  \begin{align}
					  r_+ &= \sqrt{r^2 + \frac{a^2}{4} - ar\cos\theta }\\
					  r_- &= \sqrt{r^2 + \frac{a^2}{4} + ar\cos\theta }
				  \end{align}
				  Therefore, by superposition
				  \begin{align}
					  V &= \frac{1}{4\pi\epsz}\pqty{
						  \frac{q}{r_+} + \frac{-q}{r_-}  
					  } \\
						&= \frac{q}{4\pi\epsz}\pqty{
						  \frac{1}{r_+} - \frac{1}{r_-}  
					  } 
				  \end{align}
				  or, explicitly,
				  \begin{equation}
					  V = \frac{q}{4\pi\epsz}\pqty{
						  \frac{1}{\sqrt{r^2 + \frac{a^2}{4} - ar\cos\theta }}
						  - 
						  \frac{1}{\sqrt{r^2 + \frac{a^2}{4} + ar\cos\theta }}
					  } 
				  \end{equation}
			\item \textbf{Electric field}
				  
				  The electric field then follows by
				  \begin{equation}
				      \vb{E} = -\grad V
				  \end{equation}
				  However, taking that directly from the potential seems mathematically
				  cumbersome and tiring, so I have elected to not do it. Instead, I will derive
				  the vector field for each point individually, and sum them at the end.
				  \begin{enumerate}
				      \item $+q$ \textbf{Electric field} 
						    
						    We start with the assumption that we have a point source at the origin. We 
							know its electric field
							\begin{equation}
								\vb{E_+} = \frac{q}{4\pi\epsz}\frac{\vu{r}}{r^2}  = \frac{q}{4\pi\epsz}\frac{\vb{r}}{r^3}
							\end{equation}
							We then \textit{translate} this point source ${a}/{2}$ units on the $+z$ direction.
							\begin{equation}
								\vb{E_+} = \frac{q}{4\pi\epsz}\frac{(\vb{r}-\frac{a}{2}\vu{z})}{r_+^3}
							\end{equation}
					  \item $-q$ \textbf{Electric field}
						    We take the same logic from the previous step but translate the negative point
							down by ${a}/{2}$, to the $-z$ direction.
							\begin{equation}
							    \vb{E}_- = -\frac{q}{4\pi\epsz}\frac{(\vb{r}+\frac{a}{2}\vu{z})}{r_-^3}
							\end{equation}
				  \end{enumerate}
				  Taking the vector sum of this results to the total electric field
				  \[
					  \vb{E} = \vb{E}_+ + \vb{E}_-
				  \]
				  Explicitly, we have
				  \begin{equation}
					  \vb{E} = \frac{q}{4\pi\epsz}\pqty{
						  \frac{\vb{r}-\frac{a}{2}\vu{z}}{r_+^3} 
						  -
						  \frac{\vb{r}+\frac{a}{2}\vu{z}}{r_-^3} 
					  } 
				  \end{equation}
			\item \textbf{Point dipole limit}
				  Now, we take the the limit as
				  \begin{align}
					  a\to 0 && q\to\infty
				  \end{align}
				  while keeping 
				  \begin{equation}
				      p = qa
				  \end{equation}
				  finite.

				  With $a\ll r$, we can take the binomial expansion of the inverse distances. 
				  \begin{align}
					  \frac{1}{r_+}&\approx \frac{1}{r}+\frac{a\cos\theta}{2r^2}  \\
					  \frac{1}{r_-}&\approx \frac{1}{r}-\frac{a\cos\theta}{2r^2}  
				  \end{align}
				  The difference of these is
				  \begin{equation}
				      \frac{1}{r_+} - \frac{1}{r_-} \approx \frac{a\cos\theta}{r^2}   
				  \end{equation}
				  We can then substitute this value for the $\frac{1}{r}$ in the potential
				  field.
			\item \textbf{Far field electric potential}
				
				  The potential field is defined as
				  \begin{equation}
					  V = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsz}{\frac{q}{r}} 
				  \end{equation}
					  We know that $\dfrac{1}{r}\to\dfrac{a\cos\theta}{r^2}$ at the limit.
				  Therefore:
				  \begin{equation}
					  V = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsz}{\frac{qa\cos\theta}{r^2}} 
				  \end{equation}
				  Since $qa=p$,
				  \begin{align}\label{eq:farfield potential}
					  V = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsz}\frac{p\cos\theta}{r^2} 
				  \end{align}
				  At the dipole limit. In vector form this is:
				  \begin{equation}
					  V = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsz}\frac{\vb{p}\cdot\vu{r}}{r^2} 
				  \end{equation}
				  or alternatively
				  \begin{equation}
					  V = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsz}\frac{\vb{p}\cdot\vb{r}}{r^3}
				  \end{equation}
			\item \textbf{Far field electric field}
				  
				  We take the negative of the gradient of (\ref{eq:farfield potential})
				  \begin{equation}
				      \vb{E} = -\grad V
				  \end{equation}
				  Expressing this explicitly (in spherical basis vectors), we have
				  \begin{equation}
					  \vb{E} = \bmqty{
						  \displaystyle \pdv{}{r}\\[10pt]
						  \displaystyle \frac{1}{r}  \pdv{}{\theta}\\[10pt]
						  \displaystyle \frac{1}{r\sin\theta}  \pdv{}{\phi}
					  }
					  \frac{1}{4\pi\epsz}\frac{p\cos\theta}{r^2} 
				  \end{equation}
				  Evaluating this, we have
				  \begin{align}
					  \vb{E} 
					  &= 
					  \frac{1}{4\pi\epsz}
					  \bmqty{
						  \displaystyle \pdv{}{r}\\[10pt]
						  \displaystyle \frac{1}{r}  \pdv{}{\theta}\\[10pt]
						  \displaystyle \frac{1}{r\sin\theta}  \pdv{}{\phi}
					  }
					  \frac{p\cos\theta}{r^2} \\
					  &=
					  \frac{1}{4\pi\epsz}
					  \bmqty{
						  \displaystyle \pdv{}{r}
						  \frac{p\cos\theta}{r^2}\\[10pt] 
						  \displaystyle \frac{1}{r}  \pdv{}{\theta}
						  \frac{p\cos\theta}{r^2}\\[10pt] 
						  \displaystyle \frac{1}{r\sin\theta}  \pdv{}{\phi}
						  \frac{p\cos\theta}{r^2}
					  }\\
					  &=
					  \frac{1}{4\pi\epsz}
					  \bmqty{
						  -\dfrac{2p\cos\theta}{r^3}\\[10pt] 
						  -\dfrac{p\sin\theta}{r^3}\\[10pt] 
						  0
					  }\\
				  \end{align}
				  Hence, in the spherical basis $(\vu{r},\vu*{\theta},\vu*{\phi})$, the electric field has the form
				  \begin{equation}
				      \vb{E} 
					  =
					  \frac{p}{4\pi\epsz r^3}
					  \bmqty{
						  2\cos\theta\\
						  \sin\theta\\
						  0
					  }
				  \end{equation}
				  In vector form, it has the expression
				  \begin{equation}
					  \vb{E}(\vb r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsz}\frac{1}{r^3}
					  \pqty{
						  3(\vb{p}\cdot\vu{r})\vu{r} - \vb{p}
					  }
				  \end{equation}
		\end{enumerate}
