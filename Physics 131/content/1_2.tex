
\subsection{The Electric Field}
	\subsubsection{Definition}
		The fundamental problem that electrodynamic hopes to solve is this:
		We have some collection of electric charges $q_1,q_2,q_3,\ldots$, called
		\textit{source charges}; What force do they exert on another charge $q_0$, called
		\textit{text charge}? The positions of the source charges are given (all as functions of time)
		and the trajectory of the test particle is \textit{to be calculated}. In general,
		both the source charges and test charge are in motion.

		The solution to this problem is facilitated by the \textbf{principle of superposition},
		which states that the interaction between any two charges is completely unnafected by
		the presence of others. That means that to determine the force on $q_0$, we must first 
		determine the force $\vb{F}_1$ due to $q_1$ alone, then $q_2$, and so on. Finally, we take 
		the vector sum of these individual forces.
		\[
			\sum\vb{F} = \vb{F}_1 + \vb{F}_2 \vb{F}_3 + \cdots
		\]
		Thus, if we can find the force on $q_0$ due to a \textit{single} source charge $q$, we 
		are, in principle, done, and the rest is just repitition.

		A priori, this seems easy. However, upon further study, we find that the force on $q_0$
		not only depends on the separation distance $r$ between the charges, but also depends on
		both their velocities and on the acceleration of $q$. Moreover, it is not those states 
		at this instant that matters, but the state that $q$ had in an earlier time, since information
		travels at a finite speed $c$, and we can only grasp what happened after the information reached 
		us.

		Therefore, in spite of the fact that the basic question (``What is the force on
		$q_0$ due to $q$?'') is easy to state, it does not pay to confront it head on; rather, we
		shall go at it by stages. In the meantime, the theory we develop will allow for the
		solution of more subtle electromagnetic problems that do not present themselves
		in quite this simple format. To begin with, we shall consider the special case
		of \textbf{electrostatics} in which all the \textit{source charges are stationary} (though the test
		charge may be moving).
	\subsubsection{Coulomb's Law}
		The force on a test charge $q_0$ due to a single point charge $q$, that is at \textit{rest} a 
		distance $r$ away, is given (based on experiments) by \textbf{Coulomb's Law},
		a fundamental principle first established by Charles-Augustin de Coulomb in 1785 using a torsion balance experiment.
		
		It is expressed as the following:
		\begin{definition}{Coulomb's Law}
			\begin{equation}
				\vb{F} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q_0 q}{r^2}\vu{r} 
			\end{equation}
		\end{definition}
		\newpage
		The constant $\varepsilon_0$ is called the \textbf{permitivity of free space}. In
		SI units, it is defined as 
		\begin{definition}{Permitivity of free space}
		    \begin{equation}
				\varepsilon_0 = \SI{8.85e-12}{C^2/Nm^2}
		    \end{equation}
		\end{definition}
		In other words, then, the force is proportional to the product of the charges,
		and inversely proportional to the square of their distances\footnote{Newton much?}.
		The vector $\vb{r}$ is the separation vector from $\vb{x}$, the location of $q$, and to $\vb{x}_0$,
		the location of the $q_0$ 
		\[
			\vb{r} = \vb{x} - \vb{x}_0
		\]
		and $r$ is the magnitude of this separation vector, $r=\norm{\vb{r}}$. The 
		force points along the line from $q$ to $q_0$; it is repulsive if $q_0$ and 
		$q$ are the same sign, and attractive if their signs are the opposite.
		
		Coulomb’s law and the principle of superposition constitute the physical input
		for electrostatics—the rest, except for some special properties of matter, is 
		mathematical elaboration of these fundamental rules.
	\subsubsection{The Electric Field}
		If we have \textit{several} point charges $q_1,q_2,q_3,\ldots,q_n$, at distances
		$r_1,r_2,r_3,\ldots,r_n$ from $q_0$, then the total force on $q_0$ is trivially
		\begin{align*}
			\vb{F} &= \vb{F}_1 + \vb{F}_2 + \vb{F}_3 + \cdots \\
				   &= \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \qty(
						\frac{q_0q_1}{r_1^2}\vu{r}_1 + 
						\frac{q_0q_2}{r_2^2}\vu{r}_2 + 
						\frac{q_0q_3}{r_3^2}\vu{r}_3 + 
					  \cdots )\\
				   &= \frac{q_0}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \qty(
						\frac{q_1}{r_1^2}\vu{r}_1 + 
						\frac{q_2}{r_2^2}\vu{r}_2 + 
						\frac{q_3}{r_3^2}\vu{r}_3 + 
					  \cdots )\\
		\end{align*}
		or
		\begin{equation}
		    \vb{F} = q_0 \vb{E}
		\end{equation}
		where
		\begin{definition}{Discrete definition of the Electric field}
			\begin{equation} \label{eq: discrete E}
				\vb{E}(\vb{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{q_i}{r_i^2}\vu{r}_i  
			\end{equation}
		\end{definition}
		$\vb{E}$ is called the \textbf{electric field} of the source charges. Notice that it is 
		a function of position $(\vb{r})$ because the separation vectors $\vb{r}$ depend on the location of 
		the field point $P$. But it makes no reference to the test charge $q$. The electric field is a vector
		quantity which varies from point to point and is determined by the configuration of 
		source charges. Physically ,$\vb{E}(\vb{r})$ is the force per unit charge that would
		be exerted on a text charge if you were to place one on $P$.
	\subsubsection{Continuus Charge Distribution}
		Our definition of the electric field (\ref{eq: discrete E}) assumes that the source
		of the fields is a collection of discrete point charges $q_i$ (Fig \ref{subfig: efield ptcharge}). If instead, the charge
		is distributed continuously over some region, the sum thus becomes an integral
		\begin{equation}
			\vb{E}(\vb{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int \frac{1}{r^2}\vu{r}\dd{q} 
		\end{equation}
		There are multiple ways where in charges can be distributed over a region.
		\begin{figure}[H]
			\centering
			\subfloat[Point charge]{
			\includegraphics[width=2in]{images/pointcharge.pdf}
			\label{subfig: efield ptcharge}
			}
			\subfloat[Line charge]{
			\includegraphics[width=2in]{images/linecharge2.pdf}
			\label{subfig: efield linecharge}
			}
			\hspace{0.5em}
			\subfloat[Surface charge]{
			\includegraphics[width=2in]{images/surfacecharge.pdf}
			\label{subfig: efield surfcharge}
			}
			\hspace{0.5em}
			\subfloat[Volume charge]{
			\includegraphics[width=2in]{images/volumecharge.pdf}
			\label{subfig: efield volcharge}
			}
			\caption{Charge distributions}
			\label{fig: charge distributions}
		\end{figure}
		If the charge is spread out along a \textit{line} (Fig \ref{subfig: efield linecharge}), with the charge-per-unit-length $\lambda$,
		then $dq = \lambda \dd{l}$, where $dl$ is a length element along the line. If the charge
		is smeared over a \textit{surface} (Fig \ref{subfig: efield surfcharge}), with charge-per-unit-area $\sigma$, then $dq = \sigma \dd{S}$,
		where $dS$ is an area element on the surface. And if the charge fills a \textit{volume} (Fig \ref{subfig: efield volcharge}),
		with charge-per-unit-volume $\rho$, then $dq = \rho \dd{V}$, where $dV$ is a volume element.
		\[
			dq \to \lambda dl \sim \sigma dS \sim \rho dV
		\]
		Thus, the electric field of a line charge is 
		\begin{equation}
			\vb{E}(\vb{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \int \frac{\lambda(\vb{r})}{r^2} \vu{r} \dd{l} ;
		\end{equation}
		For a surface charge, we have
		\begin{equation}
			\vb{E}(\vb{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \int \frac{\sigma(\vb{r})}{r^2} \vu{r} \dd{S} ;
		\end{equation}
		And for a volume charge
		\begin{definition}{Continuum definition of the Electric Field}
			\begin{equation} \label{eq: cont E}
				\vb{E}(\vb{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \int \frac{\rho(\vb{r})}{r^2} \vu{r} \dd{V} ;
			\end{equation}
		\end{definition}
		Equation \ref{eq: cont E} is often times referred to as ``Coulomb's Law'' because it is such
		a short step from the original, and because a volume charge is in a sense the most general
		and realisitic case.
