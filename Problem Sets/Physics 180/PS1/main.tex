	\section*{
		Problem 1.1 
	}
	\begin{problem}
		Go to \texttt{arXiv} and have a look at the relevant subjects 
		for this course (nucl-ex, nucl-th, hep-ex, hep-ph, hep-th).
		\begin{enumerate}
			\item What do the first four digits of the \texttt{arXiv} number 
				  unique to a specific paper stand for?
			  \item What are the \texttt{arXiv} numbers for the papers shown
				  in Slides 22 and 23.
			\item Cite a single paper that fascinates you within the dates
				  \textbf{August 11-20, 2026}, and briefly explain why you
				  find this particular paper interesting.
		\end{enumerate}
	\end{problem}
	\begin{solution_pset}
		\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.}]
			\item The first four numbers in an \texttt{arXiv} paper stand for the year and 
				  month that the paper is published. The numbering system goes like 
				  \[
					  \texttt{arXiv:YYMM:NNNNN}
				  \]
				  where \texttt{YY} is the last two digits of the year, and \texttt{MM} is
				  the month of the submission. As such, majority of the recent 
				  \texttt{arXiv} submissions in the time of writing has the identifier code
				  \texttt{arXiv.2608.NNNNN}. The rest of the numbers identify the paper 
				  within that month's submission sequence.
			\item The paper shown in slides 22 and 23 have the following \texttt{arXiv} identifier numbers:
				  \begin{itemize}
					  \item ATLAS Run 2 searches for electroweak production of supersymmetric particles interpreted within the pMSSM  
							\[ \texttt{arXiv.2402.01392}\text{(hep-ex)} \]
					  \item	Multi-Higgs boson probes of the dark sector
							\[ \texttt{arXiv.1912.02204}\text{(hep-ph)} \]
					  \item Hitting two BSM particles with one lepton-jet: Search for a top partner decaying to a dark photon, resulting in a lepton-jet.
							\[ \texttt{arXiv.2112.08425}\text{(hep-ph)} \]
				  \end{itemize}
			\item A particular paper that I found interesting is \texttt{arXiv.2608.13206}(gr-qc)\cite{ning2026}, entitled 
				  ``Long-term 3+1 simulations of primordial black hole formation during radiation domination" by Ning et al. This interests
				  me, well, because my field primarily right now is GR and I'm studying under Dr. Vega, so papers regarding 
				  general relativity and cosmology are of high interest to me. Reading the paper, they deal with the 
				  mathematics of primordial blackholes in 3+1 dimensions, and simulates the evolution of the collapse of such black holes during the early universe.
				  and how it behaves near-critical collaps and how it evolves post-formation, and explicitly studies non-spherical
				  symmetry. Very interesting indeed. I myself believe that primordial black holes were abundant in the 
				  early universe and is possibly one of the contributors of dark matter, so this paper is right up my
				  alley. It's pretty neat!
		\end{enumerate}
	\end{solution_pset}

	\section*{
		Problem 1.2
	}
	\begin{problem}
		Go to \texttt{InSpire} and search for all papers from 1967. 
		What is the most highly-cited paper from that year? 
		Skim through its introduction. Why do you think is it 
		highly cited?
	\end{problem}
	\begin{solution_pset}
		The most cited paper in \texttt{InSpire-HEP} in 1967 is \href{https://journals.aps.org/prl/pdf/10.1103/PhysRevLett.19.1264}{``A Model of Leptons"} by Steven Weinberg \cite{weinberg1967leptons}, which is also 
		one of the most cited papers in physics of all time. The paper's influence appears to be from its groundbreaking proposal that leptons can 
		be described by gauge fields and that bosons can acquire mass through spontaneous symmetry breaking, which is a revolutionary concept at the time.
		Skimming through the introduction, it describes a model in which the symmetry between the electromagnetic and weak interactions is 
		spontenaeously broken, but Goldstone bosons (which is massless) are avoided. It introduces the photon and intermediate
		boson fields as gauge fields.  

		Looking past that, and reading even more papers about it, this paper is probably widely cited because it introduces an elegant model which unifies the electromagnetic
		and weak forces into what we now call the electroweak force, which is one of the stepping stones towards a unified theory 
		of nature. It also shows how the W boson can acquire mass, and this paper led to testable predictions regarding the 
		masses of the W and Z bosons, the existence of weak neutral currents mediated by the Z boson, and also predicted the 
		existence of a new particle which would give mass to other particles, later known as the Higgs boson. 
		These predictions are then confirmed in the following decades. And once the theory has been proven to be renormalizable
		(which the paper at its conclusion, wasn't really sure about), and once it was known to be compatible
		with the theory of the strong force, it became the foundation of the standard model.

		Revolutionary content in just three pages. Insane.
	\end{solution_pset}

	\section*{
		Problem 2.1 
	}
	\begin{problem}
		The $B/A$ plot looks like it peaks at a specific value of $A$. Using the curve 
		approximated by SEMF, find the value of $A$ that achieves this and find 
		the peak value. What is the element that achieves this?
		\begin{center}
			\includegraphics[width=3in]{images/BAplot.png}
		\end{center}
	\end{problem}
	\begin{solution_pset}
		From \cite{flores2026nuclear}, we have the Bethe-Weizs\"acker or SEMF formula.
		\begin{equation}
			B = a_1 A - a_2 A^{2/3} - a_3\frac{Z^2}{A^{1/3}} - a_4 \frac{(Z - A/2)^2}{A} \pm a_5 A^{-1/2}
			\label{eq: SEMF formula}
		\end{equation}
		Dividing by $A$, we have
		\begin{equation}
		    \frac{B}{A} = a_1 - a_2 A^{-1/3} - a_3\frac{Z^2}{A^{4/3}} - a_4 \frac{(Z - A/2)^2}{A^2} \pm a_5 A^{-3/2}
		\end{equation}
		We aim to maximize $B/A$ with respect to $A$. Note, however
		that the function is both a function of $A$ and $Z$, so the resulting equation will be dependent of $Z$. 
		An important point here is that $Z$ is dependent of $A$.
		Since we wish to know which isobar $Z$ gives the mosty stable element, 
		we must first maximize $B/A$ with respect to $Z$. This is similar to tbe operation performed inclass about finding $N/Z$,
		but we find the stability ratio for $Z/A$ multiplied by $A$.

		We find $Z(A)$ for which $Z$ gives the most tightly bound isobar. Ignoring the pairing term for
		the smooth SEMF curve, we have
		\begin{equation}
			\pdv{B(Z,A)}{Z}) = 0
		\end{equation}
		Therefore, we have
		\begin{align}
			\pdv{}{Z} B &= \pdv{}{Z} \qty(a_1 A - a_2 A^{2/3} - a_3\frac{Z^2}{A^{1/3}} - a_4 \frac{(Z - A/2)^2}{A} \pm a_5 A^{-1/2})\\
			\pdv{}{Z} B &= - 2 a_3 \frac{Z}{A^{1/3}} - 2 a_4 \frac{(Z - A/2)}{A} \\
			0 &= - 2 a_3 \frac{Z}{A^{1/3}} - 2 a_4 \frac{(Z - A/2)}{A} 
		\end{align}
		We multiply the expression with $-A/2$ to simplifiy
		\begin{align}
			0 &= a_3 Z A^{2/3} + a_4 \qty(Z - \frac{A}{2} )\\
			0 &= a_3 Z A^{2/3} + a_4 Z - \frac{a_4 A}{2}\\
			Z(a_3  A^{2/3} + a_4 ) &= \frac{a_4 A}{2}
		\end{align}	
		Thus, we have the most stable $Z$ value for a given $A$ 
		\begin{equation}
			Z(A) = \frac{a_4 A}{2 \qty(a_3 A^{2/3} + a_4) }
			\label{eq: ZA}
		\end{equation}
		We substitute this back to $B/A$.
		\begin{equation}
		    \frac{B}{A} = a_1 - a_2 A^{-1/3} - a_3\frac{Z(A)^2}{A^{4/3}} - a_4 \frac{(Z(A) - A/2)^2}{A^2} \pm a_5 A^{-3/2}
		\end{equation}
		from this, we get
		\begin{equation}
		    \frac{B}{A} = 
				a_1 
				- a_2 A^{-1/3} 
				- a_3\frac{\qty( \frac{a_4 A}{2 \qty(a_3 A^{2/3} + a_4) })^2}{A^{4/3}} 
				- a_4 \frac{\qty( \frac{a_4 A}{2 \qty(a_3 A^{2/3} + a_4) }) - A/2)^2}{A^2} 
				\pm a_5 A^{-3/2}
		\end{equation}
		This simplifies to the following
		\begin{equation}
		    \frac{B}{A} = 
				a_1 
				- a_2 A^{-1/3} 
				- a_3 \frac{a_4^2 A^{2/3}}{4(a_3 A^{2/3} + a_4)^2} 
				- a_4 \frac{a_3^2 A^{4/3}}{4(a_3 A^{2/3} + a_4)^2}  
				\pm a_5 A^{-3/2}
		\end{equation}
		Combining the $a_3$ and $a_4$ terms, we have
		\begin{align}
				- a_3 \frac{a_4^2 A^{2/3}}{4(a_3 A^2/3 + a_4)^2} 
				- a_4 \frac{a_3^2 A^{4/3}}{4(a_3 A^2/3 + a_4)^2}  
				&= -\frac{a_3a_4A^{2/3} (a_4 + a_3 A^{2/3}) }{4 (a_3 A^{2/3} + a_4)^2} \\
				&= -\frac{a_3a_4A^{2/3} }{4 (a_3 A^{2/3} + a_4)} 
		\end{align}
		Hence,
		\begin{equation}
			\frac{B}{A} = 
			  a_1 
			- a_2 A^{-1/3} 
			- \frac{a_3a_4A^{2/3} }{4 (a_3 A^{2/3} + a_4)} 
			\pm a_5 A^{-3/2} 
		\end{equation}
		Now, we maximize this in terms of $A$. We have\footnote{At this point I just used mathematica to solve for this shit}
		\begin{align}
			\pdv{}{A}\qty(\frac{B}{A})  
				&= 
					\frac{a_2}{3} A^{-4/3} 
					- \frac{a_3a_4}{4}\pdv{}{A}\qty[\frac{A^{2/3}}{a_3A^{2/3}+a_4}] 
					\mp \frac{3a_5}{2} A^{-5/2}  \\ 
				&= 
					\frac{a_2}{3} A^{-4/3} 
					- \frac{a_3a_4^2}{6}  \frac{A^{-1/3}}{(a_3A^{2/3}+a_4)^2} 
					\mp \frac{3a_5}{2} A^{-5/2}   
		\end{align}
		This maximum satisfies
		\begin{equation}
			\frac{a_2}{3} A^{-4/3} 
			- \frac{a_3a_4^2}{6}  \frac{A^{-1/3}}{(a_3A^{2/3}+a_4)^2} 
			\mp \frac{3a_5}{2} A^{-5/2}   
			= 0
		\end{equation}
		For a smooth SEMF curve, we usually drop the pairing term, giving us
		\begin{equation}
			\frac{a_2}{3} A^{-4/3} 
			- \frac{a_3a_4^2}{6}  \frac{A^{-1/3}}{(a_3A^{2/3}+a_4)^2} 
			= 0
		\end{equation}
		This can then be further simplified by multiplying by $6A^{4/3}$
		\begin{equation}
		    2a_2 - \frac{a_3a_4^2 A}{(a_3A^{2/3}+a_4)^2} 
		\end{equation}
		Therefore
		\begin{equation}
			2a_2(a_3A^{2/3} + a_4)^2 = a_3 a_4^2 A
		\end{equation}
		If we solve for $A$, we end up with this quartic equation if $A=x^3$, basically a fractional power.
		This has no trivial closed form analytic solution.
		\begin{equation}
			2a_2 a_3^2 A^{4/3} - a_3 a_4^2 A + 4a_2 a_3 a_4 A^{2/3} + 2a_2a_4^2 = 0
		\end{equation}
		We solve for this numerically. I just used the Newton-Raphson zero finding algorithm in \texttt{C}. 
		Code is provided on the appendices to show how that works (see Listing \ref{lst:nr}). 

		Anyways, the resulting number for stable $A$, using the provided coefficients\cite{flores2026nuclear}
		\begin{align*}
			a_1 &= \SI{15.56}{MeV}\\
			a_2 &= \SI{17.23}{MeV}\\
			a_3 &= \SI{0.697}{MeV}\\
			a_4 &= \SI{93.14}{MeV}\\
			a_5 &= \SI{12}{MeV}
		\end{align*}
		Numerically solving for $A$ results to the value  $A \approx 62$.
		Using the derived equation (\ref{eq: ZA}), we can calculate the most stable
		$Z$ value for this $A$, and this results to $Z \approx 27$, or, \textbf{Nickel-62}.
		\begin{emphasis}
			\begin{align}
				N &\approx 62\\
				Z &\approx 27
			\end{align}
		\end{emphasis}
		The relevant graph is provided in the appendices (see Fig. \ref{fig: BA_plot}), compared with actual binding energy per nucleon \cite{ame2020}. While I expected something akin to $\ce{^{56}_{26}Fe}$, turns out,
		when you derive the actual energies, the actual answer is also $\ce{^{62}_{27}Ni}$, since it has the highest nuclear binding energy per nucleon. 
		However, Iron-56 is more commonly called the most stable end-product of stellar nucleosynthesis because it 
		has the lowest mass per nucleon and is produced in massive quantities by stars \cite{baird2024moststable}
		
		So hopefully this passes by the checker. Nickel and Iron are just beside each other on the periodic table. $\ce{^{62}_{27}Ni}$ is valid.

	\end{solution_pset}
